# "Primes Are Basis Vectors" — Narrator Script
# Video: 004 — Arithmetic as Linear Algebra (Primes as Basis)
# Duration: ~1:45 (105s) · 9:8 aspect, black background

============================================================
[0:00–0:12] — Hook / the fundamental theorem
============================================================
So... take a number. Any positive number, like 60. You can always break
it down into its prime building blocks — 2 twos, a 3, and a 5. Works for
literally every positive integer, and it works uniquely — no two different
ways to do it. Mathematicians call this the Fundamental Theorem of
Arithmetic, but honestly? That name undersells it, because what it's really
telling us is something almost scandalous: the primes aren't just a
curiosity. They're the atomic elements that every whole number is secretly
made of.

============================================================
[0:12–0:34] — Primes as building blocks
============================================================
Look — 12 is really just a couple of 2s and a 3. 18? Same ingredients,
shuffled. 100? Just 2s and 5s in the right amounts. Every number you can
think of is just primes, multiplied together in the right proportions.

Now here's the thought that keeps me up at night... what if the exponents
didn't have to be positive? What if they could be any integer at all —
could we then describe every positive fraction, every positive rational
number, with the exact same system? Let's hold that thought. For now, set
it aside.

============================================================
[0:34–0:46] — But first: what's a basis?
============================================================
Because this setup — small ingredients that combine to build everything
else — should feel familiar if you've ever poked at linear algebra. In
three-dimensional space, we have these three "basis vectors": e1 shoots
along one direction, e2 along another, e3 up the third. And the entire
trick of the whole space is that any point, any vector at all, is just "a"
times the first arrow, plus "b" times the second, plus "c" times the third.
The basis gives you three building blocks that can reach everywhere.

============================================================
[0:46–1:05] — THE aha — primes as coordinates
============================================================
But wait. Look at your numbers again. Sixty equals 2², times 3¹, times 5¹.
Now watch what happens if I treat the primes like those basis vectors —
and pull the exponents out, and drop them into coordinate slots. Indexed
by the primes: 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, and so on, forever.

Then 60 becomes the tuple (2, 1, 1, 0, 0, …). The primes are the axes. The
exponents are the coordinates. Every integer is secretly just a list of
prime-coordinates, most of which are zero.

============================================================
[1:05–1:25] — Multiplication becomes addition
============================================================
Now let's multiply. Take 12 — that's (2, 1, 0, …). And 18 — that's
(1, 2, 0, …). Multiply them together and you get 216, which factors as
2³ times 3³. So look at those coordinate rows when you multiply:
(2,1,0) plus (1,2,0) — they just ADD to give (3,3,0).

Multiplication, this whole time... was just addition in disguise. The
moment you view numbers through the lens of the primes, multiplying
becomes adding the exponents.

============================================================
[1:25–1:40] — The pattern crystallizes
============================================================
So a pattern is forming, and it's not subtle. In the prime-world: addition
maps to multiplication. Exponentiation maps to scalar multiplication. And
the primes... the primes are the basis. It's a perfect mirror of linear
algebra — a vector space hiding inside ordinary arithmetic.

============================================================
[1:40–2:00] — The catch: can it be a vector space?
============================================================
But wait. If this is really a vector space, then 60 should have coordinates
and there should be scalars to scale those coordinates with... but here's
the rub. The exponents don't go down — they only grow: 0, 1, 2, 3, and up.
And if we try to take a step backwards? A negative exponent like 2 to the
minus 1, that's one-half — and now we've been pushed clean out of the
integers, into fractions.

============================================================
[2:00–2:20] — Positive rationals to the rescue
============================================================
Which is fine — because that was the whole question we shelved earlier!
Let negative exponents in, and suddenly fractions are just numbers with a
few negative coordinates. Twelve twenty-fifths is 2² times 3¹ times 5⁻² —
that's (2, 1, −2, 0, …).

And the payoff is enormous: every positive rational number — every fraction
you've ever met — is just a finitely-supported list of integer coordinates.
No exceptions. The whole set of positive rationals slots perfectly into
this coordinate system.

============================================================
[2:20–2:40] — The climax: not a vector space, but a module
============================================================
So the big question: is this actually a vector space? Almost — the
coordinates are the integers, the natural coordinate object is ℤ⁽ᴺ⁾, the
direct sum of ℤ over the primes. But here's what makes a vector space a
vector space: you need scalars that can be divided. And the integers are
not a field — you can't divide 1 by 2 and still be an integer. So this
isn't a vector space. It's something a little more humble and a lot more
elegant: a FREE ℤ-module.

============================================================
[2:40–2:52] — The punchline
============================================================
And that's the resolution: ℚ positive is isomorphic to the direct sum of ℤ
from every prime — which is just a mathematician's way of saying the primes
are a basis of the arithmetic of fractions. Not a vector space, but a free
ℤ-module, where the operation is multiplication and the basis is the primes.
The two things you learned years apart — prime factorization and linear
algebra — were the same idea all along.

============================================================
[2:52–3:00] — The hook
============================================================
Which, of course, leaves me wondering... what happens if we just CHANGED
the scalars? What if the exponents could be arbitrary? But that's a story
for another video.
